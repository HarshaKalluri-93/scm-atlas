What Is What — In Plain English
New to supply chain? Read this first. Every major topic on this site explained in two lines — no jargon, no fluff.
🌐
Foundation
Supply Chain Management (SCM)
The end-to-end process of getting a product from raw material to the customer's hands. It covers sourcing, production, warehousing, transport, and everything in between.
🤝
Upstream
Procurement & Sourcing
Finding, evaluating, and buying from suppliers. Think of it as the "shopping" function of a business — but at scale, with contracts, negotiations, and quality controls.
📦
Operations
Inventory Management
Making sure you always have the right amount of stock — not too much (wastes money) and not too little (loses sales). It's the art of keeping shelves full without overspending.
🚢
Logistics
Logistics & Transportation
Moving goods from A to B — by sea, air, road, or rail. Logistics decides the fastest, cheapest, and most reliable way to get products where they need to go.
🏭
Operations
Warehouse Management
Running the building where your stock lives. It covers how goods are received, stored, picked, packed, and dispatched — and the software (WMS) that controls it all.
✈️
Trade
Imports & Exports
Buying goods from another country (import) or selling them to one (export). Both involve paperwork, customs checks, and rules about what can cross which borders.
🛃
Trade
Customs
The government checkpoint that inspects goods crossing borders. Customs decides what duty (tax) is owed and whether the goods are allowed into the country at all.
🏷️
Trade
Duty & Tariffs
A tax charged by a government when goods enter a country. The rate depends on what the product is (its HS code) and where it came from. Higher duty = more expensive imports.
🆓
Trade
Duty Free & Free Trade Zones
Ways to legally avoid or delay paying import duty. Bonded warehouses, Free Trade Zones, and trade agreements all let businesses move or store goods without paying the full duty upfront.
📋
Trade
Incoterms
A set of 11 internationally agreed rules that define who — buyer or seller — is responsible for paying freight, insurance, and customs at each stage of a shipment. FOB and DDP are the most common.
🛒
Digital
E-Commerce
Selling products online through a website, app, or marketplace. It connects directly to supply chain because every online order needs to be picked, packed, and delivered — fast.
🔧
Digital
WooCommerce
A free plug-in for WordPress that turns a website into an online shop. It's one of the world's most popular e-commerce platforms, used by small businesses to large retailers to sell and manage orders online.
🔗
Digital
Dropshipping
Selling products you don't physically own or stock. When a customer orders, you forward the order to a supplier who ships directly to the customer — you never touch the product.
🗄️
Technology
ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning)
The central software brain of a business. ERP connects finance, purchasing, inventory, sales, and production in one system so everyone is working from the same data.
📡
Technology
WMS (Warehouse Management System)
Software that runs a warehouse — it tells staff what to receive, where to put it, what to pick, and how to pack it. Think of it as the warehouse's operating system.
🚛
Technology
TMS (Transportation Management System)
Software that plans, books, and tracks freight. A TMS finds the best carrier and route for each shipment, generates shipping documents, and monitors deliveries in real time.
🏢
Logistics
3PL (Third-Party Logistics)
A company you hire to handle your warehousing, fulfilment, or freight instead of doing it yourself. DHL, XPO, and Kuehne+Nagel are examples. Saves capital but means less direct control.
📈
Planning
Demand Forecasting
Predicting how much customers will buy in the future so you can order the right amount of stock. Good forecasting prevents both empty shelves and costly overstock.
🎯
Methodology
Lean
A philosophy that focuses on eliminating waste — any step, material, or time that doesn't add value to the customer. Made famous by Toyota, now used in warehouses, factories, and offices worldwide.
📊
Methodology
Six Sigma
A data-driven method for reducing errors and inconsistency in any process. The goal is near-perfect quality — fewer than 3.4 defects per million opportunities.
🔄
Methodology
Kaizen
Japanese for "continuous improvement." It's the idea that small, daily improvements made by everyone in the team add up to massive results over time. No change is too small.
🚦
Operations
OTIF (On Time In Full)
A key supply chain KPI — did the order arrive on time AND with everything ordered? It's the gold standard measure of delivery performance for both suppliers and logistics providers.
🔢
Trade
HS Code (Harmonised System)
A universal 6-digit number that classifies every product traded internationally. Customs uses it to determine the correct duty rate. Getting it wrong can mean paying the wrong tax — or having your shipment held.
📄
Trade
Bill of Lading (B/L)
The most important document in sea freight — it's both the contract between shipper and carrier and the title deed for the goods. Whoever holds the original B/L legally owns the shipment.

Import Process Step by Step
Customs
1
Goods Arrive at Port — Carrier notifies importer; goods placed in temporary storage (T1).
2
Customs Entry Filed — Broker submits declaration with HS code, origin, value, Incoterm.
3
Duty & VAT Assessed — Customs calculates: CIF value × duty rate + import VAT.
4
Payment or Deferment — Pay immediately or use duty deferment account (pay monthly).
5
Release & Delivery — Customs stamps clearance; freight released to importer or 3PL.

🗺️ What is Transport Planning?
Transport planning is the process of deciding how, when, and with whom goods will move from one location to another. It sits at the intersection of procurement (buying freight capacity), operations (scheduling and execution), and customer service (delivery promises). Done well, it reduces cost, improves reliability, and keeps customers happy. Done poorly, it creates delays, premium costs, and strained relationships.
Core Objective
Move the right goods
To the right place, at the right time, in the right condition, at the lowest total cost — consistently and reliably across every lane and every carrier.
Who Does It
Transport / Logistics Team
In-house logistics planners, freight forwarders, 3PL partners, or a combination. Supported by TMS software and carrier management tools.
Key Input
Demand Signal
Sales orders, warehouse despatch plans, or production schedules tell transport what needs to move, when, and where — this is the trigger for all planning activity.
Key Output
Load Plan + Carrier Booking
A confirmed booking with a carrier, a load plan matching cargo to vehicle, and documentation ready for despatch. This is what makes the truck move.
🚛 How Trucks Are Booked — The Full Process
Truck booking isn't a single action — it's a workflow that starts with a demand signal and ends with a confirmed, documented load assignment. Here's every step.
STEP 01
Demand Signal
Sales order confirmed or despatch plan released. Logistics team receives the requirement: what, how much, where to, by when.
›
STEP 02
Load Building
Planner groups orders by destination, weight, and volume. Decides: FTL (full truck) or LTL (part load). Optimises cube utilisation.
›
STEP 03
Carrier Selection
Check contracted carrier for the lane. If capacity unavailable, go to spot market. Rate card vs spot decision made here.
›
STEP 04
Booking Placed
Formal booking sent to carrier via TMS, portal, email, or EDI. Collection date, time window, location, and reference confirmed.
›
STEP 05
Carrier Confirmation
Carrier accepts and assigns a vehicle and driver. Confirmation reference issued. SLA clock starts from this point.
›
STEP 06
Docs Prepared
Despatch note, CMR/consignment note, delivery instructions, and any special handling notes generated and ready for driver.
›
STEP 07
Collection & Departure
Driver arrives, goods loaded, seals applied (if required), paperwork signed. Transport leg begins. Tracking activated.
💡 CONTRACTED vs SPOT Contracted rates are pre-agreed for specific lanes for 6–12 months — predictable cost, guaranteed capacity. Spot rates are one-off market prices — flexible but volatile. Best practice: 70–80% contracted, 20–30% spot buffer for flexibility.
📦 Load Types — FTL, LTL, Groupage & More
The type of load you book determines your cost, transit time, and risk. Choose based on volume, urgency, and budget.
FTL
Full Truck
You fill the entire vehicle
Best for high volume. Direct route, no stops, fastest. ~33 pallets on a standard 13.6m trailer.
LTL
Part Load
Your cargo shares the truck
Cost-effective for smaller shipments. Slower — multiple collection/delivery stops. Good for 2–15 pallets.
Groupage
Consolidated
Many shippers, one truck
Freight forwarder combines loads from multiple clients into one vehicle. Most economical for small volumes.
Dedicated
Dedicated Fleet
Your own contracted vehicles
Carrier reserves trucks exclusively for you. Reliable, branded, higher cost. Suits daily high-volume routes.
Express
Express / Urgent
Same day or next day
Premium cost — typically 3–5× standard rate. Used for urgent replenishment, production-critical parts, or service failures.
Intermodal
Intermodal
Road + rail combined
Container moves by rail for long legs, road for first/last mile. Lower carbon, cost-effective on long corridors (e.g. 500km+).
📋 The Carrier Bidding Process — RFQ to Contract
Every 6–12 months, most logistics teams run a formal carrier tender to benchmark rates, secure capacity, and optimise their carrier mix. Here's how it works from start to finish.
PHASE 1
Spend Analysis
Pull last 12 months of freight data. Map by: lane, mode, carrier, volume, weight, spend. Identify top 80% of spend — these are your priority lanes for the tender.
PHASE 2
Lane Definition
Define each lane: origin postcode/zone → destination postcode/zone. Include volume forecast (weekly pallets/loads), weight range, and service requirement (next day, 2-day, etc.).
PHASE 3
RFQ Issue
Send Request for Quotation to 4–8 shortlisted carriers. Include: lane data, volume forecast, service KPIs required, contract term, and pricing template. Set 2-week response deadline.
PHASE 4
Bid Evaluation
Score each bid on: rate per lane, transit time commitment, tracking capability, insurance limits, financial stability, and past performance. Not just cheapest — best value.
PHASE 5
Negotiation
Top 2–3 carriers per lane invited to negotiate. Lever: volume commitment, payment terms, multi-lane bundling, contract length. Never reveal competitor rates.
PHASE 6
Award & Contract
Primary and secondary carrier awarded per lane. Contract signed with rate card, KPIs, SLAs, penalty/bonus clauses, and notice period. Go-live date confirmed.
Best Practice
Always dual-award key lanes
Award each critical lane to a primary carrier (70–80% volume) and a secondary (20–30%). If primary fails or capacity runs out, secondary activates immediately — no scrambling for spot trucks.
Evaluation Tip
Rate is only one score
A carrier 5% cheaper but with poor tracking, high damage rates, or weak financial health costs more in the long run. Weight your scorecard: 40% rate, 30% service, 20% technology, 10% financial health.
Timing
Run tenders Q3 for Q1 start
Q4 is peak freight season — rates spike and carrier capacity tightens. Lock contracts before peak. Starting a tender in October means competing for attention with every other shipper doing the same thing.
Key Document
Rate Card + SLA Schedule
The contract backbone. Rate card sets the price per lane/weight break. SLA schedule defines: transit time, POD turnaround, damage thresholds, and financial remedies for failures.
💻 Transportation Management System (TMS)
A TMS is the software that plans, executes, and monitors freight. It replaces spreadsheets and phone calls with automated carrier selection, booking, tracking, and cost management.
📥
Function 1
Load Planning
Automatically groups orders into optimal loads based on destination, weight, volume, and delivery date. Reduces manual planning time by 60–80%.
🔍
Function 2
Carrier Selection & Rate Shopping
For each load, TMS automatically selects the best carrier from the contracted rate card. For spot loads, it can broadcast to a carrier marketplace and select the best live bid.
📡
Function 3
Real-Time Tracking
Live GPS tracking of vehicles, milestone updates (collected, in-transit, out for delivery, delivered), and automated alerts for delays or exceptions.
📄
Function 4
Document Generation
Auto-generates despatch notes, consignment notes (CMR for road), carrier labels, and POD requests. Eliminates manual document preparation errors.
💰
Function 5
Freight Invoice Audit
Automatically matches carrier invoices against contracted rates. Flags overcharges, duplicate invoices, and billing errors. Industry average: 3–8% of freight invoices contain errors.
📊
Function 6
Performance Analytics
Carrier scorecards, lane cost analysis, on-time performance trends, CO₂ per shipment, and cost-per-kg benchmarks. The data that drives carrier reviews and tender preparation.

👥 Who Does What — Transport Planning Roles
Understanding who owns each part of the transport process is essential for cross-functional coordination.
Role
Transport Planner
Builds daily load plans, books carriers, coordinates collection windows with warehouse. Owns the day-to-day execution of freight movements.
Role
Freight / Logistics Manager
Owns carrier relationships, runs tenders, manages contracts and rate cards, reviews KPIs, and escalates service failures. Strategic owner of the transport function.
Role
Freight Forwarder
External specialist who arranges international freight on your behalf — books space with carriers, handles customs documentation, and manages the end-to-end shipment. Your agent in the market.
Role
Carrier / Haulier
The company that physically moves the goods. Employs drivers, owns or leases vehicles. May be a large network carrier (DHL, XPO) or an independent owner-operator.
Role
Customs Broker
Handles import/export declarations at borders. Classifies goods with correct HS codes, calculates duties, and ensures compliance. Essential for international shipments.
Role
Dispatcher / Control Tower
Monitors all live shipments in real time. Manages exceptions — delays, breakdowns, missed collections. Proactive communication to customers when issues arise.

🤝 Handoffs — Where Shipments Actually Break
A shipment rarely fails in the middle of a motorway. It fails at a handoff — the moment responsibility, custody, or data passes from one party to another. Every handoff is a control point.
Handoff 1
Warehouse → Driver
Custody transfers when the driver signs the loading document. Common failure: driver signs "unchecked" or "STC" (said to contain), which weakens your claim if quantities are later disputed. Always get a piece-count signature.
Handoff 2
Carrier → Sub-Contractor
Many carriers sub-contract legs without telling you. Visibility often drops the moment they do. Contractually require notification of sub-contracting and insist tracking continues through the sub-carrier.
Handoff 3
Trunk → Local Depot
Groupage freight is broken down and re-sorted at a hub. This is where pallets get mis-sorted onto the wrong local vehicle. Clear, durable labelling on every pallet face is the single best defence.
Handoff 4
Freight Forwarder → Customs Broker
Documents pass from forwarder to broker for the declaration. If the commercial invoice, packing list, and HS codes do not match exactly, the entry is rejected and the shipment sits. Data quality, not physics, causes the delay.
Handoff 5
Driver → Receiving Bay
The receiving site checks and signs. A "clean" POD closes your liability. A POD signed with damage or shortage notations opens a claim — which is why the notation window (often 3–7 days) matters so much.
Handoff 6
Physical → Financial
POD triggers invoicing. If PODs are slow or missing, cash is trapped. Chasing POD turnaround is a finance issue disguised as a logistics one — set a contractual POD SLA (e.g. 24–48 hours, digital).

📄 Documents That Travel With the Goods
Physical goods move with a paper (or digital) shadow. Missing or mismatched documents stop shipments at borders, at gates, and at receiving bays. Expand any document to understand what it is and who issues it.
CMR / Consignment Note — road freight
+

The contract of carriage for international road transport under the CMR Convention. It travels with the driver in three signed copies: sender, carrier, and consignee.

Why it matters
  • It is your evidence. Damage or shortage noted on the CMR at delivery is the basis of any claim against the carrier.
  • Liability is capped. CMR limits carrier liability by weight (SDR per kg), not by cargo value — high-value goods need separate cargo insurance.
  • Clean vs claused. A "clean" CMR means goods received in apparent good order. Any notation makes it "claused" and preserves your claim rights.
Who issues it

The sender (shipper) prepares it; the carrier and consignee sign it at each stage.

Bill of Lading (B/L) — sea freight
+

The most powerful document in trade. It does three jobs at once: receipt for goods, contract of carriage, and document of title — meaning whoever holds the original B/L controls the cargo.

Key variants
  • Original (negotiable) B/L — must be physically surrendered to release cargo. Used where payment security matters (letters of credit).
  • Seaway Bill — non-negotiable, no original needed. Faster release, used between trusted parties or intercompany moves.
  • Telex Release / Express Release — shipper surrenders originals at origin, carrier releases at destination without paper. Avoids the classic "cargo arrived, documents didn't" problem.
Practitioner note

Demurrage charges accrue while you wait for an original B/L to arrive by courier. If payment terms allow, always push for seaway bill or telex release.

Air Waybill (AWB) — air freight
+

The air equivalent of the B/L — but crucially, the AWB is never a document of title. It is a receipt and contract only. Cargo is released to the named consignee on identification, not on surrender of paper.

  • MAWB vs HAWB. The Master AWB is issued by the airline to the forwarder. The House AWB is issued by the forwarder to you. Consolidated shipments have one MAWB covering many HAWBs.
  • Liability is governed by the Montreal Convention and capped per kilogram — again, insure separately for value.
  • Speed trade-off. Air is typically 5–10× the cost of sea per kg but cuts weeks to days. Justified for high-value, perishable, or production-critical parts.
Packing List & Commercial Invoice
+

The two documents customs authorities scrutinise most closely — and the two most often inconsistent with each other.

Commercial invoice

States the parties, goods description, HS codes, quantities, unit and total value, currency, country of origin, and the Incoterm. This is the basis for duty and VAT calculation.

Packing list

States how the goods are physically packed: number of cartons/pallets, gross and net weight, dimensions, and marks and numbers. Used for physical verification at inspection.

The number one rejection cause

Values, weights, piece counts, or descriptions that do not reconcile between the two documents. Build a single source of truth in your ERP and generate both from it — never key them separately.

Proof of Delivery (POD) & Delivery Note
+

The delivery note lists what should be in the consignment. The POD is the signed confirmation of what was actually received — and it is the trigger for invoicing.

  • Clean POD — signed with no notations. Carrier liability ends. Invoice can be raised with confidence.
  • Claused POD — signed "1 pallet short" or "2 cartons crushed". This preserves your claim. Train receiving teams never to sign clean when there is visible damage.
  • ePOD — driver app captures signature, photos, and GPS/time stamp. Turnaround drops from days to minutes and photo evidence dramatically improves claim recovery rates.
ASN, Dangerous Goods Note & Certificates
+
ASN (Advance Shipping Notice)

An electronic message sent ahead of the truck telling the receiving site exactly what is coming, on which pallets, arriving when. Retailers and large manufacturers mandate it — without a valid ASN, your delivery may be refused at the gate or charged a non-compliance fee.

DGN (Dangerous Goods Note)

Required for hazardous cargo under ADR (road), IMDG (sea), or IATA DGR (air). Declares UN number, class, packing group, and handling requirements. Misdeclaration is a serious offence, not an admin error.

Certificates
  • Certificate of Origin — proves where goods were made; unlocks preferential duty under trade agreements.
  • Phytosanitary / Health certificate — for plants, food, animal products.
  • Temperature log — for cold chain; a break in the log can invalidate the entire load.

🏭 Warehouse Types — Choosing the Right Node
A warehouse is not just a building with shelves. The type of facility you use determines your cost structure, your speed to customer, your duty position, and how much control you retain. Most networks combine several types.
Type 01
Distribution Centre (DC)
High-throughput facility built for fast movement, not long storage. Receives bulk inbound, breaks it down, and ships out orders daily. Measured on throughput and dwell time, not on how full it is.
Type 02
Fulfilment Centre
Optimised for high-volume single-item and small-parcel picking (e-commerce). Dense pick faces, packing benches, carrier sortation. Very different layout economics to a pallet-out DC.
Type 03
Bonded Warehouse
Customs-approved facility where imported goods are stored with duty and import VAT suspended until released to free circulation. Enormous cash-flow benefit for slow-moving imported stock, and duty-free if re-exported.
Type 04
Cross-Dock Facility
Minimal or zero storage. Inbound goods are unloaded, sorted, and reloaded onto outbound vehicles within hours. Cuts inventory holding entirely — but demands near-perfect inbound timing and data.
Type 05
3PL / Shared-User Site
You rent space and labour from a logistics provider, sharing the building with other clients. Converts fixed cost to variable, scales with your volume, but you have less control over priority at peak.
Type 06
Cold Store / Temperature Controlled
Chilled (0–5 °C), frozen (−18 °C or below), or ambient-controlled. Far higher cost per pallet, strict hygiene and traceability regimes, and every dock door needs a sealed, temperature-protected interface.
Type 07
Dark Store / Micro-Fulfilment
Small urban node positioned close to customers for same-day or next-hour delivery. Trades cost efficiency for speed. Replenished nightly from the main DC.
Type 08
Overflow / Buffer Store
Cheap, low-service space used for seasonal peaks, promotional builds, or slow-moving stock. Watch it carefully — buffer stores are where obsolete inventory quietly accumulates (see Block 5).

The dock door is the narrowest point in the whole supply chain. It is a fixed-capacity resource shared by every inbound and outbound vehicle, and it is where transport meets warehouse. Manage it badly and you pay twice — in detention charges to carriers and in idle labour inside.

📊 Why Planning Sits Upstream of Transport
Blocks 1–3 covered execution. But almost every expensive transport problem — the air freight, the emergency truck, the half-empty trailer — is created weeks earlier by a planning decision. This block explains the planning machinery that generates the demand your transport team has to serve.
The Link
Planning creates the freight
Every purchase order, production order, and stock transfer order that planning releases becomes a shipment someone has to book. Planning quality directly determines freight cost and load efficiency.
The Symptom
Premium freight is a planning KPI
If your expedite spend is climbing, the root cause is rarely the transport team. It is late supplier orders, a bad forecast, or a safety stock set too low. Track premium freight back to its planning trigger.
The Lever
Order sizing sets load fill
Order quantities set without reference to pallet or container fill produce trailers running at 60% capacity. Aligning lot sizes to a full pallet, full layer, or full container is one of the fastest freight savings available.
The Discipline
One number, one plan
Sales, operations, and finance working from different numbers guarantees either stockouts or excess. S&OP exists to force a single agreed plan that everyone downstream — including transport — executes against.
🧭 The Planning Hierarchy — From Strategy to the Truck
Planning happens at different horizons, each feeding the next. Confusing the layers is a common source of organisational argument — a question about next week is not answered by a process designed for next year.
Horizon: 1–5 years
Strategic / Network Planning
Where should warehouses sit? Which markets do we serve from where? Make or buy? Decides the physical shape of the network — and therefore the structural cost of every future shipment.
Horizon: 3–18 months
S&OP / IBP (Sales & Operations Planning)
A monthly cross-functional cycle that reconciles the demand plan with supply capability and the financial plan. Output is a single agreed volume plan by product family. Escalates gaps to executives for a decision.
Horizon: 1–6 months
Demand Planning / Forecasting
Statistical baseline forecast plus market intelligence, promotions, and new product launches. Measured on forecast accuracy and bias. This is the primary input that everything downstream depends on.
Horizon: 1–13 weeks
MPS (Master Production Schedule)
Converts the demand plan into a buildable schedule of finished goods by week, constrained by capacity. Answers: what will we actually make, and when?
Horizon: daily / weekly run
MRP (Material Requirements Planning)
Explodes the MPS through the bill of materials to work out what components must be ordered or made, in what quantity, and by when. Generates the purchase and production order proposals.
Horizon: daily
DRP (Distribution Requirements Planning)
Plans replenishment between warehouses in a multi-echelon network. Generates the stock transfer orders — which become the internal freight the transport team books.
Horizon: today / tomorrow
Execution — Transport & Warehouse
Blocks 1–3. Load building, carrier booking, picking, docking, despatch. This layer can only be as good as the plan it inherits.

📐 Lot Sizing & Safety Stock — The Two Levers That Move Freight
Two parameter choices, made once and often never revisited, silently determine your load fill, your order frequency, and your expedite rate.
Lot-sizing rules — how much to order at once
+
  • Lot-for-Lot (L4L) — order exactly what is needed. Minimum inventory, maximum order frequency and freight cost. Suits expensive, bulky, or short-shelf-life items.
  • Fixed Order Quantity — always order the same amount (often a full pallet, layer, or container). Simple, predictable, and freight-efficient, but can carry excess.
  • Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) — mathematically balances ordering cost against holding cost. Elegant, but assumes stable demand and often ignores transport constraints.
  • Period Order Quantity — cover a fixed number of weeks of demand per order. Adapts to demand changes while keeping order frequency stable.
  • Min–Max — order up to a maximum whenever stock falls below a minimum. Common for consumables and spares.
The transport connection

A lot size of 470 units when a pallet holds 120 means you ship three full pallets and one pallet with ten units on it — paying full pallet freight for a nearly empty pallet. Rounding lot sizes to transport-friendly quantities routinely cuts freight cost several percent with zero service impact.

Safety stock — insurance against variability
+

Safety stock exists to absorb two kinds of uncertainty: demand being higher than forecast, and supply arriving later than promised. Set it too low and you buy air freight; set it too high and you fund a warehouse full of cash.

What actually drives the right level
  • Demand variability — how erratic the item's consumption is, not how large it is.
  • Lead time variability — usually the bigger driver. A supplier with a 30-day lead time that is always exactly 30 days needs far less cover than one averaging 20 days but ranging 10 to 40.
  • Target service level — the cost curve is brutally non-linear. Moving from 95% to 99% availability can nearly double safety stock.
Common failure

Setting safety stock as a flat "four weeks for everything". It over-stocks stable, cheap items and under-protects the volatile, critical ones. Segment first (ABC by value, XYZ by variability), then set policy per segment.

Lead time — the parameter nobody maintains
+

MRP works backwards from the need date using the lead time in the item master. If that number is wrong, every order it generates is wrong — usually late.

  • Total lead time is more than supplier lead time. It includes order approval, supplier production, transit, customs clearance, and goods-in processing. Many item masters only capture the middle piece.
  • It drifts. Suppliers change, routes change, ports congest. Lead times set at go-live and never reviewed are the single most common cause of chronic late orders.
  • Fix: run a quarterly report comparing actual PO receipt dates against planned dates, and update the item master from real data rather than from what the supplier claims.
MRP nervousness — why the plan keeps changing
+

"Nervousness" is when a tiny change at the top of the plan causes large, cascading changes to order quantities and dates further down — orders appearing, disappearing, and moving between runs. Planners lose trust in the system and start overriding it manually, which makes things worse.

Standard controls
  • Time fences — freeze the near horizon (e.g. no changes inside 2 weeks), allow changes only beyond it.
  • Firming — convert planned orders to firm planned orders so MRP stops rescheduling them.
  • Damping / rescheduling tolerance — ignore date changes smaller than a threshold (e.g. under 3 days).
  • Exception-based working — planners review only the messages that matter rather than re-checking the whole plan every run.
👥 Planning Teams — Who Owns What
In large organisations these are separate teams; in smaller ones one person wears several of these hats. The roles are the same either way.
Role
Demand Planner
Owns the forecast. Builds the statistical baseline, layers in promotions and launches, and challenges sales input. Measured on forecast accuracy (MAPE) and bias — not on whether the number is optimistic.
Role
Supply / Materials Planner
Runs MRP, reviews exception messages, converts planned orders to purchase orders, and chases suppliers on confirmations and late deliveries. The most operationally intense planning role.
Role
Production Planner / Scheduler
Owns the master production schedule and the detailed line sequence. Balances capacity, changeover time, and material availability against the promised dates.
Role
Inventory / Replenishment Planner
Sets and maintains safety stock, reorder points, and lot sizes. Owns DRP between warehouses and the stock transfer orders that create internal freight. Guardian of working capital.
Role
S&OP / IBP Manager
Runs the monthly cycle, facilitates the cross-functional meetings, and prepares the gap scenarios and decisions for the executive review. A process and influence role more than an analytical one.
Role
Master Data Owner
Unglamorous and critical. Maintains lead times, MOQs, pack sizes, BOMs, and planning parameters. Bad master data quietly breaks every downstream plan, and nobody notices until orders are late.
🗓️ The Monthly S&OP Cycle
A repeatable five-step rhythm. The dates shift by company but the sequence is near-universal.
WEEK 1
Data Gathering
Close last month's actuals. Measure forecast accuracy, service level, and inventory. Refresh the statistical baseline forecast for the horizon.
WEEK 2
Demand Review
Sales, marketing, and demand planning agree the unconstrained demand plan. Promotions, launches, and lost-business risks are quantified and assumptions documented.
WEEK 3
Supply Review
Operations tests the demand plan against capacity, material availability, and supplier constraints. Produces a constrained plan and a list of gaps.
WEEK 4
Pre-S&OP
Cross-functional team reconciles demand vs supply vs finance. Builds scenarios and recommendations for each gap. Resolves what it can; escalates what it cannot.
WEEK 4/5
Executive S&OP
Leadership makes the trade-off decisions — invest capacity, accept shortfall, reprioritise customers. The output is the single approved plan everyone executes.
💡 THE TEST OF A REAL S&OP If the executive meeting is a presentation of numbers with no decisions made, it is a reporting meeting wearing an S&OP badge. A functioning cycle brings two or three genuine trade-offs to the table each month and closes them with a named owner and a date.

⚠️ Exception Management — The Work Behind the Work
In a mature operation, 90–95% of shipments run without intervention. The whole cost and reputation of the function is decided by how well the remaining 5–10% is handled. Exception management is not firefighting — it is a defined process with triggers, owners, timeframes, and a feedback loop back into planning.
Principle 1
Detect before the customer does
The difference between a managed exception and a service failure is who found out first. Automated milestone alerts and predictive ETA flags exist so you can call the customer before they call you.
Principle 2
Pre-agree the playbook
Decide in advance what happens for each exception type: who is notified, within how long, who authorises expedite spend, and up to what value. Deciding this during a crisis wastes the hours that matter most.
Principle 3
Code every exception
Log a structured reason code, not a free-text note. Reason codes are what let you see that 40% of your delays trace to one carrier or one supplier — patterns free text will never reveal.
Principle 4
Close the loop upstream
An exception resolved but not fed back is an exception that recurs. Monthly review of reason-code data should change carrier awards, safety stock, lead times, or supplier terms.

📑 Freight Claims — How Recovery Actually Works
Most companies recover far less than they are entitled to, because claims fail on evidence and timing rather than on merit.
Step 01 — At delivery
Note the exception on the POD
A clean signature is close to fatal to a claim. Receiving teams must write the specific damage or shortage on the POD and CMR before signing, and photograph it in place before anything is moved.
Step 02 — Within 24–72 hours
Give formal written notice
Conventions and carrier terms set short notification windows — often 3 days for visible damage and 7 for concealed damage under CMR-type regimes. Miss the window and the claim is usually time-barred regardless of the facts.
Step 03 — Preserve
Keep the goods and the packaging
Do not destroy or return damaged goods until the carrier or insurer has had the opportunity to inspect. Retain packaging — it often shows whether damage was caused by handling or by inadequate packing, which decides liability.
Step 04 — Quantify
Build the claim file
Commercial invoice, packing list, POD/CMR with notations, photographs, damage report, and a costed statement of loss. Remember liability is usually capped by weight, not value — so a light, high-value load may recover only a fraction.
Step 05 — Submit & track
Claim against carrier or cargo insurer
Carrier liability claims are limited and slow; a cargo insurance policy typically pays full value faster and then pursues the carrier itself. For valuable goods, insure — do not rely on carrier liability.
Step 06 — Feed back
Use claims data in carrier reviews
Claim frequency and value per carrier belongs on the quarterly scorecard and in the next tender. A carrier who is cheap but damages 1% of pallets is not cheap.
⚖️ LIABILITY IS CAPPED BY WEIGHT Under international road and air conventions, carrier liability is limited to a set amount per kilogram of the goods lost or damaged — not their commercial value. A pallet of electronics and a pallet of sand of equal weight attract the same maximum recovery. This is exactly why cargo insurance exists.
🗑️ Obsolete & Excess Stock (E&O) — How It Builds Up
Obsolete stock is rarely created by one bad decision. It accumulates quietly through a series of individually reasonable choices, and it is usually discovered by finance rather than by operations.
Cause 01
Product changeover without run-down
A new version launches while old stock and old components remain. Without a formal run-down plan agreed before launch, the old stock becomes instantly unsellable.
Cause 02
Forecast that never happened
An optimistic launch or promotion forecast triggers a large build. Sales come in at a fraction. The excess sits, ages, and eventually breaches shelf life or relevance.
Cause 03
MOQ and bulk-buy discounts
A supplier price break for a year's worth of a slow-moving component looks like a saving. Two years later it is a write-off plus the storage cost of holding it.
Cause 04
Engineering change
A spec change makes existing components incompatible. Without checking stock on hand and on order before approving the change, the change itself creates the obsolescence.
Cause 05
Returns and customer rejections
Returned stock lands in a quarantine area and stays there because nobody owns the disposition decision. Quarantine areas are where inventory goes to be forgotten.
Cause 06
Customer-specific stock
Bespoke or private-label items built for one customer become worthless the moment that customer leaves. Always negotiate a stock liability clause in the contract before building.

Companies often carry heavily provisioned stock for years because nobody owns the physical disposal decision — the loss is already taken, but the space, handling, and counting cost continues.
🔄 Reverse Logistics — The Flow That Runs Backwards
Returns, recalls, repairs, and packaging all move against the normal direction of flow. Reverse volumes are typically a fraction of forward volumes but consume disproportionate handling effort, because every returned item is a one-off requiring a decision.
STEP 01
Return Authorisation
Customer requests a return. An RMA number is issued with a reason code. No RMA, no receipt — unauthorised returns are the main source of unidentifiable stock.
›
STEP 02
Collection
Reverse freight is booked, often as backhaul on an existing outbound route to avoid paying full price for a near-empty return leg.
›
STEP 03
Receipt & Quarantine
Goods booked in against the RMA into a segregated quarantine location. Critically, they are not yet available to sell.
›
STEP 04
Inspection & Grading
Each item graded: A (as new, resaleable), B (repackable), C (repairable), D (scrap). Grading rules must be written down or grading drifts with whoever is on shift.
›
STEP 05
Disposition
Restock, rework, return to supplier, sell as refurbished, or scrap. A hard SLA here (e.g. 5 working days) is what stops quarantine becoming a graveyard.
›
STEP 06
Credit & Close
Customer credited, stock revalued, reason code logged. Reason-code analysis feeds product quality, packaging design, and listing accuracy improvements.

WHAT IS LEAN? Lean is a management philosophy, born on the Toyota production line, built on one idea: relentlessly remove anything that doesn't add value for the customer, so work flows faster and cheaper. It doesn't ask "how do we work harder?" — it asks "what are we doing that the customer would refuse to pay for?" and then removes it.

💡 LEAN IN SCM CONTEXT Lean applied to supply chains means: eliminating non-value-added touches in order processing, reducing supplier lead times, cross-docking to remove warehousing steps, and designing supplier networks that flow rather than batch-and-queue. A single-piece-flow mindset applied to order fulfilment can cut lead time by 60–80%.
WHAT IS SIX SIGMA? Six Sigma is a data-driven, statistical method for cutting variation and defects out of a process until output becomes almost perfectly predictable — the name refers to a defect rate of just 3.4 per million opportunities. Where Lean asks "what's wasted?", Six Sigma asks "why does this process behave inconsistently, and how do we prove the fix worked with data?" It runs on the DMAIC cycle (see the DMAIC tab).

WHAT IS KAIZEN? Kaizen ("change for the better" — 改善) is the philosophy that improvement should be continuous, small, and done by the people doing the work, not handed down as a one-off project. Where a Six Sigma project might run for months with a Black Belt leading it, a Kaizen improvement can be a five-minute idea implemented on the same shift. It's the cultural engine that keeps Lean and Six Sigma gains from eroding over time.

WHAT IS TQM? Total Quality Management is the idea that quality isn't one department's job — it's embedded into every process, owned by everyone, driven from the top, and never "finished." Where Six Sigma is a statistical toolkit for one process and Lean is about flow and waste, TQM is the culture and management system both often sit inside.
The Founders
TQM
Deming
14 Points & PDCA. Core belief: most defects come from the system, not the worker — fix the system.
Juran
The Juran Trilogy — Quality Planning, Quality Control, Quality Improvement. Quality = "fitness for use."
Ishikawa
Invented the Fishbone diagram; championed Quality Circles — small teams solving their own problems.
Crosby
Coined "Zero Defects" and "Quality is Free" — prevention costs less than fixing failures.

6

Early in an investigation, when the cause is unknown and you want to avoid tunnel vision on the first guess. A structured map of candidate causes to investigate or rule out with data.
5 Whys Repeatedly asks "why" (typically five times) to drill from a symptom down to its root cause. Fast, simple problems with one dominant cause chain — a daily Kaizen habit, not for complex multi-variable issues. A single traced root cause and a corrective action.
Pareto Chart Ranks causes/defects by frequency to show the 80/20 split — which few causes drive most of the problem. You have a list of many possible issues and limited time — need to prioritise which to fix first. A ranked "vital few vs trivial many" list to focus effort on.
FMEA Failure Mode & Effects Analysis — scores each potential failure by severity × occurrence × detection before it happens. Proactively, before launching a new process, supplier, or product — risk-proofing rather than firefighting. A ranked risk register with mitigation actions for the highest-risk failure modes.
A3 A single-page structured story: background → current state → root cause → target → countermeasures → follow-up. Communicating a problem-solving effort to leadership concisely, or coaching a team through structured thinking. One-page documentation that aligns the team and sponsor on the plan.
SIPOC Suppliers-Inputs-Process-Outputs-Customers — a high-level, one-page view of an entire process before diving into detail. Start of any project (especially DMAIC "Define") to agree scope before anyone maps low-level detail. Agreed process boundaries and stakeholder list.
VSM Value Stream Map — draws every step of a process with time and value data, separating value-add from waste. You need to see the whole end-to-end flow (not just one step) to find where delay or handoffs pile up. Current-state and future-state maps showing where to cut lead time.
Control Chart / SPC Plots a metric over time against statistically-derived control limits to separate normal variation from a real signal. After a fix is implemented — to prove it's sustained (DMAIC "Control"), or to monitor an ongoing process for drift. An early-warning chart flagging when a process goes out of control.
Quick Decision Guide
Compare
Slow
Process works but takes too long / too many touches → Lean (VSM to find and cut waste)
Unreliable
Output quality is inconsistent, error rates high → Six Sigma (DMAIC to find root cause statistically)
Both
Slow AND unreliable → Lean Six Sigma, the default for most major initiatives
Quick win
Small, obvious, low-cost fix needed today → Kaizen (don't open a project for it, just fix it)
One bottleneck
A single stage is capping total output → TOC (fix the constraint before anything else)
Culture-wide
Quality problems span multiple departments → TQM (needs leadership-level sponsorship)
Changing fast
Requirements shift before a full project can finish → Agile (iterate in short cycles)
